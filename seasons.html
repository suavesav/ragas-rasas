The Six Ritu

Season in raag theory, and how little of it survived into performance

The Hindu calendar divides the year into six seasons, not four, and the medieval treatises assigned a raag to each. Almost none of that system is still observed. Two seasons kept their music — the spring raags and the monsoon Malhars — and the rest passed out of practice, so that a modern musician who would never dream of singing Bhairav in the evening will sing it in any month of the year. This page separates what is still a rule from what is a historical association, because the app treats them differently: season is a rule, and an association is a link the sources record without prescribing.

The six seasons

Each ritu spans two lunar months. Vasant is spring, roughly mid-February to mid-April; Grishma the hot season before the rains; Varsha the monsoon; Sharad the clear autumn after it; Hemant the early winter of the harvest; Shishir the cold end of the year that runs into spring again.

The treatise scheme

The raga-ragini schemes of the fourteenth to nineteenth centuries were built on six patriarchal raags, each with five or six raginis. The Raga Guide reproduces the version attributed to Hanuman and given by Damodara (c. 1625): Bhairav, Kaushik (Malkauns), Hindol, Dipak, Shri and Megh. Those same six were mapped onto the six seasons. The Archives and Research Centre for Ethnomusicology gives the most widely followed mapping, drawn from the Sangita Ratnakara (13th century) and the Sangita Darpana (c. 1625):

It is not the only scheme. Deepak Raja gives an older one from the Manasollasa of Someshwara (1131), which assigns spring to Vasanta, summer to Bhairava, monsoon to Megh, autumn to Panchama, early winter to Natanarayana and deep winter to Shree. The two agree on almost nothing: only Megh's monsoon and Shree's winter survive the comparison, which is exactly why those two are the season assignments this app treats as rules. Bhairav moves from autumn to summer between the schemes, and two of the Manasollasa's raags are no longer performed at all.

What survived

Deepak Raja puts it plainly: "the enthusiasm of the performing tradition has been limited largely to Vasanta (spring) and Varsha (rainy season)." He suggests why — both seasons are ones in which nature renews itself and brings relief, spring from the cold and the monsoon from the heat, so both carry a festival calendar and a body of song. Writing in The Tribune, Shailaja Khanna reports the same thing from the other end: the autumn, pre-winter and summer raag traditions have "passed into oblivion," and one vocalist she quotes calls seasonal raag theory "a fable now."

What that leaves is two living traditions and a handful of documented but unobserved links. The Raga Guide reflects this exactly: of its seventy-four raags, it gives a season in the time field for only six — Bahar and Basant for spring, Gaud Malhar, Megh, Miyan ki Malhar and Sur Malhar for the rains — plus Shree for winter and a note that the hori compositions in Kafi belong to Holi.

Raags in this app, by season

A raag appears under rule where two independent sources give it that season. It appears under association where the link is recorded but not prescriptive: a treatise assignment no longer observed, a single source, or a connection that comes from thumri and film rather than from classical practice.

Why the summer raags are thin

Grishma's raag was Dipak, which legend says set lamps alight and burned the singer, and which is effectively extinct: Parrikar reports it as "all but dead", with no agreement even on its scale. What is left of a summer association attaches to the noon raags rather than to a season proper. Vrindavani Sarang is the one raag in this app with a corroborated summer link — tanarang writes that its note combinations "clearly show the intensity of noon time and the summer season to which the melody is particularly oriented", and Wikipedia gives summer as its season citing Yagnik. The other Sarangs, and Bhimpalasi, Multani and Madhuvanti, are afternoon raags with no sourced season; the summer wording that used to sit on them in this app was a gloss on the hour, not a citation.

A caution

Season, like time of day, is a convention rather than an acoustic fact. The Raga Guide's own framing is that the association of a raag "with a specific emotional state, a season or time of day, though intangible, is as relevant as its melodic structure" — relevant, that is, as culture and as expectation, not as a rule the notes enforce. Nothing in the scale of Miyan ki Malhar makes rain fall. What makes it a monsoon raag is four centuries of songs about clouds.
